# Atmospheric Dryness and Evaporative Demand in Southeastern Türkiye (1990–2025)

## A Long-Term Assessment of Vapor Pressure Deficit, Potential Evapotranspiration, and Hydroclimatic Variability

---

## Abstract

Atmospheric dryness represents an important but frequently underexamined component of hydroclimatic variability. Conventional drought assessments commonly emphasize precipitation deficits; however, atmospheric demand for water is also strongly controlled by temperature, humidity, radiation, and wind conditions.

This study investigates atmospheric dryness and evaporative demand in Southeastern Türkiye during the 1990–2025 period. The analysis uses climatic data obtained from the NASA Prediction of Worldwide Energy Resources (NASA POWER) database and evaluates temperature, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, wind speed, and surface solar radiation.

Two principal indicators are emphasized: Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD) and Potential Evapotranspiration (PET). VPD is used to characterize atmospheric moisture demand and atmospheric dryness, whereas PET represents the potential evaporative demand of the atmosphere.

The analytical framework incorporates Mann–Kendall trend analysis, Sen's slope estimation, Pettitt change-point detection, seasonal assessment, and climatic correlation analysis.

The results provide an integrated perspective on atmospheric dryness and evaporative demand and demonstrate the importance of considering atmospheric water demand alongside precipitation when evaluating hydroclimatic variability and potential environmental stress.

# 1. Why Was This Study Conducted?

## Scientific Motivation

Drought is traditionally evaluated through precipitation deficits.

However, precipitation alone cannot fully describe atmospheric water stress.

The atmosphere continuously demands water through evapotranspiration, and this demand can increase as a consequence of:

- Increasing temperature
- Decreasing relative humidity
- Increasing solar radiation
- Changes in wind speed
- Increasing evaporative demand

Consequently, atmospheric dryness may increase even when precipitation trends alone do not adequately explain changes in environmental water stress.

This study was therefore conducted to investigate the **atmospheric component of hydroclimatic change** in Southeastern Türkiye.

# 8. Why VPD Is Important

## Vapor Pressure Deficit

Vapor Pressure Deficit represents the difference between the amount of moisture the atmosphere can hold at saturation and the amount of moisture actually present.

It is therefore an important indicator of atmospheric moisture demand.

### General interpretation

**Low VPD**

→ relatively moist atmospheric conditions

**High VPD**

→ dry atmospheric conditions

**Increasing VPD**

→ increasing atmospheric moisture demand

High VPD conditions can increase transpiration demand and potentially intensify vegetation and agricultural water stress when water availability is limited.

---

# 9. Why PET Is Important

## Potential Evapotranspiration

Potential Evapotranspiration represents the atmospheric demand for evapotranspiration when sufficient water is available.

PET is influenced by:

- Temperature
- Relative humidity
- Solar radiation
- Wind speed

Therefore, PET provides an integrated indicator of atmospheric evaporative demand.

# 12. Mann–Kendall Trend Test

The Mann–Kendall test is a non-parametric method used to evaluate monotonic temporal trends.

It provides information regarding whether a variable exhibits a statistically meaningful increasing or decreasing tendency.

The method is particularly appropriate for hydroclimatic time series because it does not require normally distributed observations.

---

# 13. Sen's Slope Estimator

Sen's slope is used to estimate the magnitude of an observed monotonic trend.

### Interpretation

**Positive Sen's slope**

→ increasing trend

**Negative Sen's slope**

→ decreasing trend

Sen's slope therefore complements the Mann–Kendall test by providing information about the rate of change.

---

# 14. Pettitt Change-Point Test

The Pettitt test is used to identify potential abrupt shifts within climatic time series.

This is important because climate variability may occur through both:

- gradual trends
- abrupt changes

The combined use of Mann–Kendall, Sen's slope, and Pettitt testing provides a more comprehensive temporal assessment.

# 25. Integrated Results

The results demonstrate that atmospheric dryness should be considered as a multi-dimensional hydroclimatic phenomenon.

Temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, radiation, and wind interact to determine atmospheric moisture demand and evaporative conditions.

VPD provides information on atmospheric moisture deficit, whereas PET provides information on potential evaporative demand.

Together, these indicators provide a more comprehensive assessment of atmospheric water demand.

# 26. Main Scientific Findings

### Finding 1 — Atmospheric dryness is multi-variable

Atmospheric dryness is not determined by precipitation alone. Temperature, humidity, radiation, and wind also contribute to atmospheric water demand.

### Finding 2 — VPD is an important dryness indicator

Higher VPD indicates greater atmospheric moisture demand and potentially greater vegetation water stress.

### Finding 3 — PET represents atmospheric evaporative demand

Higher PET indicates stronger potential atmospheric demand for evapotranspiration.

### Finding 4 — Temperature is an important climatic control

Temperature influences atmospheric moisture capacity and contributes to changes in VPD and PET.

### Finding 5 — Trend and change-point analyses are complementary

Mann–Kendall and Sen's slope evaluate gradual changes, whereas Pettitt analysis identifies potential abrupt shifts.
