# Atmospheric Dryness and Evaporative Demand in Southeastern Türkiye (1990–2025)

## A Spatiotemporal Assessment of Vapor Pressure Deficit, Potential Evapotranspiration, Climate Variability, and Atmospheric Water Demand

---

# 1. Research Overview

Atmospheric dryness is an important component of hydroclimatic variability because the atmosphere can exert increasing demand for water even when changes in precipitation alone do not fully indicate the magnitude of environmental water stress.

This research investigates the long-term variability of atmospheric dryness and evaporative demand in Southeastern Türkiye during the **1990–2025** period.

The study focuses particularly on:

- Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD)
- Potential Evapotranspiration (PET)
- Air temperature
- Maximum temperature
- Minimum temperature
- Relative humidity
- Precipitation
- Wind speed
- Surface solar radiation

The project combines climatic indicators with statistical trend and change-point analyses to provide an integrated assessment of atmospheric water demand.

---

# 2. Why Was This Study Conducted?

## Scientific Motivation

Drought is traditionally evaluated through precipitation deficits. However, precipitation alone does not describe the complete atmospheric water balance.

Atmospheric demand for water can increase as a consequence of:

- Increasing temperature
- Decreasing relative humidity
- Increasing evaporative demand
- Changes in wind speed
- Changes in available solar energy

Therefore, a region may experience increasing atmospheric dryness even when precipitation trends do not fully explain the observed environmental stress.

This study was designed to investigate this additional dimension of hydroclimatic change.

---

## Regional Importance

Southeastern Türkiye is particularly relevant because the region contains extensive agricultural areas, semi-arid environments, and water-dependent ecosystems.

Changes in atmospheric moisture demand may influence:

- Agricultural water requirements
- Irrigation demand
- Soil moisture
- Vegetation stress
- Evapotranspiration
- Ecosystem functioning
- Surface-water demand
- Groundwater dependence
- Drought development

Understanding atmospheric dryness is therefore important for both physical geography and environmental management.

7. Statistical Methodology
7.1 Mann–Kendall Trend Test

The non-parametric Mann–Kendall test is used to evaluate monotonic temporal trends.

The method is appropriate for climatic time-series analysis because it does not require the observations to follow a normal distribution.

The analysis focuses on identifying:

Trend direction
Statistical significance
Temporal persistence of climatic changes
7.2 Sen's Slope Estimator

Sen's slope is used to estimate the magnitude of monotonic temporal trends.

The method provides a robust estimate of the rate of change and complements the Mann–Kendall significance test.

Interpretation:

Positive slope → increasing trend
Negative slope → decreasing trend
Larger absolute slope → greater magnitude of change

For VPD and PET, positive trends may indicate increasing atmospheric moisture demand and evaporative demand.

7.3 Pettitt Change-Point Test

The Pettitt test is used to detect potential abrupt changes in the temporal distribution of climatic variables.

This method complements monotonic trend analysis by identifying possible transitions between different climatic regimes.

The combination of:

Mann–Kendall + Sen's Slope + Pettitt

allows gradual trends and abrupt shifts to be evaluated together.

7.4 Seasonal Analysis

Seasonal variability is evaluated to distinguish recurring intra-annual patterns from long-term climatic behaviour.

This is particularly important for atmospheric dryness because VPD and PET can exhibit strong seasonal cycles.

7.5 Correlation Analysis

Correlation analysis is used to investigate relationships among the principal climatic variables.

The analysis focuses on relationships between:

Temperature
Relative humidity
Precipitation
VPD
PET
Wind speed
Solar radiation

Pearson correlation provides information about linear relationships, whereas Spearman correlation evaluates monotonic associations.

9.3 Vapor Pressure Deficit

VPD integrates atmospheric temperature and moisture conditions and therefore represents an important indicator of atmospheric dryness.

Figure 3. Vapor Pressure Deficit

Scientific interpretation:
Higher VPD corresponds to greater atmospheric moisture demand. Persistent increases in VPD can therefore indicate a tendency toward stronger atmospheric drying conditions.

VPD is particularly relevant for vegetation, agriculture, and ecosystem studies because high atmospheric demand can increase plant transpiration and water requirements.

Potential Evapotranspiration provides an estimate of atmospheric evaporative demand.

PET provides a complementary indicator to VPD by representing the potential atmospheric demand for water through evapotranspiration.

9.6 Long-Term Trend Analysis

The Mann–Kendall test and Sen's slope estimator provide complementary information about long-term climatic trends.

Figure 6. Mann–Kendall and Sen's Slope Analysis

Scientific interpretation:

The Mann–Kendall framework evaluates whether a statistically meaningful monotonic trend is present, while Sen's slope quantifies the direction and magnitude of that trend.

10. Integrated Results Interpretation

The combined results demonstrate that atmospheric dryness is a multi-dimensional hydroclimatic phenomenon.

Temperature, humidity, precipitation, wind speed, and solar radiation jointly influence atmospheric moisture demand and evaporative conditions.

The simultaneous assessment of VPD and PET provides a more comprehensive representation of atmospheric water demand than precipitation-based analysis alone.
12. Key Scientific Findings
Finding 1 — Atmospheric Dryness Is Multi-Dimensional

Atmospheric dryness cannot be adequately characterized using precipitation alone.

VPD provides information about atmospheric moisture demand, while PET provides information about potential evaporative demand.

Finding 2 — Temperature Is a Major Control

Temperature strongly influences saturation vapor pressure and therefore contributes to changes in VPD.

This makes long-term temperature variability an important component of atmospheric-dryness assessment.

Finding 3 — Evaporative Demand Is Critical

PET provides an important indicator of the potential demand for water by the atmosphere.

Changes in PET may influence agricultural irrigation requirements and ecosystem water demand.

Finding 4 — Climatic Variables Interact

Atmospheric dryness results from the interaction of:

Temperature
Humidity
Precipitation
Radiation
Wind

Consequently, multivariable assessment provides a more complete representation of hydroclimatic conditions.

Finding 5 — Trend and Change-Point Analyses Are Complementary

Mann–Kendall evaluates monotonic trends.

Sen's slope quantifies the magnitude of trends.

Pettitt identifies potential abrupt changes.

Together, these methods provide a stronger statistical framework for climatic time-series assessment.
